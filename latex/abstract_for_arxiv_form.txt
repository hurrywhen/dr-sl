Cloud-local LLM inference forwards a de-identified artifact to a cloud model only after a local release gate approves it. We audit whether such approval can be trusted, studying one concrete runtime-sensitive-set (runtime-S) release chain on frozen synthetic benchmarks; conclusions are scoped to this audited implementation and do not estimate failure rates of other gates or real deployments. Three core findings: (1) missed extraction causes false release — the chain releases 57.5% of a shared-vocabulary holdout, yet 78.3% of released instances (Wilson [64.4,87.7]) retain gold sensitive literals, and banking releases 20/20 with residue in every case; (2) the utility gate rewards unknown task coverage — when no task-relevant attribute is extracted, utility falls back to context retention, so worse extraction makes release easier (65% of releases pass through this harmful fallback, all leaking); a coverage gate routing such cases to humans eliminates false release on this holdout at a large release-rate cost; (3) the closed loop adds no benefit over one-shot deterministic generalization — paired release and leakage differences are zero on three holdouts, and downstream reference-answer preservation shows no detectable mean difference. A constructive fully-disjoint-vocabulary stress test and a dose-response series quantify vocabulary dependence; fixing clear implementation defects removes false release on shared-vocabulary data but not under vocabulary shift, separating software bugs from structural blind spots. We correct a tied-rank Spearman error and rerun a contaminated downstream experiment, and contribute a reproducible audit framework with conditional false-release metrics, release/domain stratification, and public raw outputs — not certified zero leakage or a superior automatic sanitizer.
